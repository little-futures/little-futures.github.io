*The question is no longer what you use, but where your work lives*

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1200/format:webp/0*VxiplYgHF8YCZyaZ.jpg)

There’s an easy way to describe what’s happening right now. It’s to say that systems like Claude are doing to software what Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter did to media.

That framing is helpful at a glance. It gives you a shape for the shift. But it starts to break down once you look more closely at how these systems actually behave.

Social platforms reorganized attention. They became the place where people discovered content, and over time, they inserted themselves between creators and audiences. Content still lived on websites. Publishers still controlled what they produced. But the path between creation and consumption was no longer direct. It ran through an intermediary that decided what was surfaced and when.

AI systems are not doing the same thing. They are not primarily organizing attention. They are organizing intent.

People are not opening Claude to browse. They are opening it to do something like write a memo, analyze data, generate a plan. The interaction starts with a goal, even if that goal is only partially formed. The system interprets that goal and moves it forward. Sometimes it points to a tool. Increasingly, it completes enough of the task that the need to open the original tool is reduced.

That does not eliminate software. But it does begin to change where software sits in the workflow.

For a long time, software products were destinations. You opened a design tool to design. You opened an analytics platform to answer questions. The interface of the product was where the work happened, and that interface defined the experience.

With AI systems, the interface starts to sit in front of that. It becomes the place where intent is expressed and interpreted. At first, this looks like a more flexible way to access existing tools. Over time, it changes how often those tools are directly engaged with. Work still flows through them, but the entry point shifts.

What matters more is what happens after the initial interaction.

When you ask a system like Claude to create something, the result is often an artifact; a document, a design, a structured output that can be edited and extended. Once that artifact exists, the system has an opportunity to keep you working inside it. You are no longer prompting continuously. You are adjusting, refining, and iterating.

There is a practical reason this pattern shows up. The economics of these systems are tied to tokens. Prompts and responses are expensive at scale, so there is a natural incentive to reduce how often they are required. Generate something once, then allow the user to work within it with minimal additional cost. Tools like Claude Design follow this model. They use a burst of tokens to create an initial output, and then allow for extended interaction that requires far fewer of them.

That creates a progression that feels small in the moment but compounds over time. A user arrives with intent, receives an output, and then stays inside an environment where that output lives. The system is not only answering a question. It is hosting the work that follows from it.

If that work remains there, the system begins to accumulate context. Not just isolated outputs, but a record of how things evolve. That continuity has value. It also introduces a different kind of lock in. It is less about features and more about where the work itself resides.

At the same time, the capabilities across AI systems are converging quickly. What one system can do, another can often replicate soon after. That puts pressure on differentiation. If the intelligence layer becomes more interchangeable, advantage shifts elsewhere. It moves toward the layer that captures and retains the work.

There is also a second effect. As it becomes easier to generate functional tools on demand, systems do not always need to route users to existing products. They can create lightweight versions of those tools directly within their own environments. [Paul Ford describes](https://aboard.com/claude-design-just-wants-you-to-stop-burning-tokens/) a future where AI platforms spin up categories of software as needed, not as standalone businesses but as extensions of user intent. In that model, software is less of a destination and more of something that appears in response to a need.

That does not mean existing platforms disappear. In many domains, especially those that are complex or high stakes, users will continue to rely on established systems. Those systems hold data, enforce controls, and support workflows that are difficult to replicate quickly. In those cases, AI may function more as an orchestration layer on top of existing state rather than the owner of it.

This is where the outcome becomes less uniform.

In some contexts, AI systems will host the work directly. In others, they will sit on top of systems that continue to own the underlying data and workflows. The balance will vary depending on how sensitive the work is, how embedded existing tools are, and how much control organizations are willing to give up.

Even so, the direction of pressure is consistent. The primary interaction layer is moving outward. It is becoming more conversational, more flexible, and less tied to a single product interface. That changes what it means for a product to be central.

A lot of current thinking leans into accessibility, to integrate with these systems and expose functionality. To be present wherever users are working. Those are necessary steps, but they are not sufficient on their own.

A product can be widely used without being the place where users feel like their work lives.

That distinction matters. Because over time, value tends to concentrate where continuity exists. Where context builds and where work persists and compounds.

The idea that the homepage is dead came out of the social era. In some ways, it captured what was happening. But it also obscured something more important. What mattered was not the page itself, but the relationship it represented. A direct connection between a system and its user, with continuity over time.

In this environment, that concept still exists. It may not look like a traditional interface. It may be a persistent layer of context that sits behind or alongside conversational entry points. But wherever the state of the work is owned, that is where the relationship ultimately resides.

Social platforms took control of what people saw. AI systems are starting to shape how people do things. The open question is where that doing lives, and who ultimately holds onto it.
